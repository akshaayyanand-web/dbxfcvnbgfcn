# Worked Examples — Winning Native Ad Copy

Two full examples of finished native ad copy in the target voice, for a children's electric toothbrush. Read whichever tone is closer to what's being asked for (confession-blog-post style vs. high-status-parent style) before drafting new copy — study the hook, the agitation detail, the single mechanism reveal, and the CTA.

## Example 1 — "European kids have fewer cavities" (confession style)

I discovered why European kids have 73% fewer cavities than American kids, and it has nothing to do with sugar—it's the TOOTHBRUSHES they're allowed to sell there.

This revelation came at 2:34 AM, after my third glass of wine, staring at my laptop screen through tears because I'd just gotten the estimate for my son Oliver's dental work.

$2,400. Six cavities. He was five.

The dentist's words kept echoing: "We're seeing this more and more. Parents doing everything right, kids still getting cavities."

Everything right? I was obsessive about Oliver's teeth. Timer apps. Fluoride toothpaste. Two minutes, twice a day. No juice. Limited candy. The expensive electric toothbrush with his favorite character.

Yet there I was, scheduling sedation dentistry for a kindergartener.

My sister in Munich had called earlier that day. Her twins, the same age as Oliver, had just had their checkups. Perfect teeth. And trust me, those kids eat plenty of Haribo.

"Maybe it's genetic," she'd offered, trying to make me feel better.

But that made no sense. We have the same genetics. Same parents. Same terrible teeth genes from Dad's side of the family.

So at 2 AM, I started digging. Not mommy blogs. Not Amazon reviews. Academic papers. European dental journals. FDA regulations versus EU regulations.

What I found made me angry. Then sick. Then angry again.

In Europe, children's toothbrushes have to meet specific efficacy standards. Not safety standards, efficacy. They have to actually prove they clean teeth before they can be sold.

In America? They just have to not hurt you.

That's it. That's the entire FDA requirement for a kids' toothbrush. Don't cause immediate harm.

I pulled up the EU database of approved children's dental products. Cross-referenced it with what's sold in America.

Almost no overlap.

The character toothbrushes dominating our shelves? Not sold in Germany. Not sold in France. Not sold in Sweden. Not approved for efficacy.

But here's what really got me: I found this Danish study comparing American kids' brushing to European kids' brushing using the exact same technique, same duration, same everything. The only difference? The tool.

American kids, using American approved brushes, removed 35% of plaque on average.

European kids, using EU approved brushes, removed 89%.

Same kids. Same technique. Same time. Different tool.

The study's conclusion was damning: "The efficacy gap between regulatory standards accounts for the majority of difference in cavity rates between populations."

Translation: Our toothbrushes suck, and that's why our kids have cavities.

I looked at Oliver's Batman toothbrush charging on the counter. The one I'd researched for hours. The one with 15,000 five star reviews. The one the mom groups recommended.

It wasn't approved in a single European country.

Not. One.

3:15 AM. Still searching. Found a leaked document from a major toothbrush manufacturer's internal meeting. They literally discussed how the American market allows them to prioritize "character licensing and marketing appeal" over "functional design" because there's no efficacy requirement.

We're choosing toothbrushes based on which cartoon our kids like, while European parents are choosing based on which actually removes plaque.

And we wonder why our kids have more cavities.

I started looking for what European kids actually use. Not what's marketed to them, what pediatric dentists there recommend.

Kept seeing references to these U shaped automatic brushes. Not the silicone garbage on Amazon, those are toys. The medical grade ones with actual nylon bristles.

The physics made sense immediately. A regular toothbrush cleans one surface at a time, relying on perfect technique and a still child. These things clean all surfaces simultaneously. No technique required. No cooperation needed.

But here's the kicker, in Europe, they've figured out you can make effective brushes that kids actually WANT to use. The one I found? Shaped like a dinosaur. Still fun for kids, but the fun part doesn't compromise the function.

See, American companies slap characters on ineffective brushes and call it a day. European companies start with efficacy, then add design elements kids love. The singing and dancing? That's just a distraction. A cool dinosaur shape with lights? That's engagement without sacrificing the engineering.

Big difference.

I found one company that makes them to EU standards but ships to America. FDA approved (meaning safe) but more importantly, CE marked for efficacy (meaning they actually work).

$39.99. Dinosaur shaped, three colors to choose from. No songs or gimmicks, but still fun enough that kids want to use it.

The design was clever, it looked like you were feeding the dinosaur your teeth. The lights weren't random, they pulsed with the cleaning cycle. Fun with purpose.

I ordered the green one. Oliver loves dinosaurs.

When it arrived, Oliver was immediately interested. "It's a T Rex!"

"A T Rex that eats plaque," I told him.

First night using it. He bit down on this U shaped thing, pressed the button, 30 seconds of vibration, done. The dinosaur "mouth" fit around his teeth perfectly. The lights made him giggle.

I used a plaque disclosure tablet right after. You know, those tablets that turn remaining plaque pink?

His teeth were clean. Actually clean. Cleaner than after our usual two minute Batman battle.

The back molars, the ones that got all the cavities, were completely pink free. First time ever.

Six months later. Dental checkup. I was physically nauseous walking in, preparing for more bad news.

The hygienist came out with a strange look. "What changed?"

No new cavities. The existing ones hadn't progressed. His teeth were... fine.

"We got a different toothbrush."

She asked to see it. I showed her a picture on my phone.

"Oh, the European style ones. Yeah, we can't recommend those officially, but..." She glanced around, lowered her voice. "That's what I use for my kids."

Wait, what?

"The ADA can only endorse products from companies that pay for endorsement. It's a business relationship, not an efficacy rating. These European companies don't play that game."

I sat in that office realizing the entire system is backwards. We're choosing our kids' health tools based on marketing budgets and cartoon licenses while actual effective products sit unknown because they don't pay for shelf space.

My sister called last week. Her twins still cavity free. She was confused why I was so interested in their toothbrush.

"It's just the standard one here," she said. "The one the kindergarten recommends."

The standard one there. That we can't even find here.

I think about that $2,400 dental bill. The sedation. The trauma. Oliver's fear of the dentist now. All because I trusted the American system. Trusted the reviews. Trusted the characters on the package.

I used the "right" toothbrush according to everything our market told me.

It was just the wrong tool according to actual science.

European parents aren't better at brushing. They're not more disciplined. Their kids don't eat less sugar.

They just have access to tools that actually work because their regulations demand it.

We're over here trusting Batman to protect our kids' teeth while the rest of the developed world uses physics and engineering.

Your kid's cavities might not be your fault. Or their fault.

It might be the tool that was never designed to actually work in the first place.

The solution isn't brushing harder or longer or with more expensive toothpaste.

It's using what actually works, even if it doesn't come from the toy aisle.

Even if you have to look beyond the big brands.

Even if it makes you realize how badly we've been played.

Oliver hasn't had a cavity in 18 months now. That's longer than he'd gone since his teeth came in.

Same kid. Same diet. Same parents.

Different tool.

The right tool.

And yes, he still calls it his "dinosaur mouth" and races his sister to see whose lights finish first. Turns out you can have fun AND function. You just can't find it at Target.

I wish I'd known the difference $2,400 ago.

## Example 2 — "The VC parent who optimizes everything" (status/efficiency style)

The pediatric dentist at our private practice pulled me aside and said, "Whatever that device is, every family in Greenwich needs one."

I'd just mentioned, casually, that bedtime takes 12 minutes now. Total. Both kids. Teeth included.

She stared at me like I'd said we'd discovered time travel.

Here's what you need to understand: I run a venture capital firm. I've optimized everything in my life. Our home runs on systems. The Tesla schedules its own service. The fridge orders groceries. Our calendar auto syncs across five devices.

But every night at 8 PM, I was still on my knees in the bathroom, manually scrubbing tiny teeth while my seven year old Parker negotiated for fewer seconds and my five year old Olivia performed interpretive dance instead of opening her mouth.

Four minutes per child. Minimum. That's 2,920 minutes a year. 48 hours of my life annually spent hunched over a sink, counting to 120 while small humans resisted.

My husband called it "character building." I called it insanity.

We have a machine that makes perfect espresso in 19 seconds, but we were using the same tooth cleaning method my grandmother used in 1955?

It bothered me. Really bothered me. Not the time itself, though losing 48 productive hours annually hurt, but the sheer primitive inefficiency of it.

I mentioned this at a partners' dinner. You should've seen the looks. Like I'd complained about having to breathe manually.

"It's just part of parenting," someone said.

But why? We've automated everything else. Why are we still manually scrubbing teeth like cave people with fancy electric sticks?

I started researching one night after the kids were asleep. Not desperately. Methodically. The same way I research potential investments.

Electric toothbrushes for kids. 1,847,000 results. All variations of the same Victorian era concept: bristles on a stick that you manually navigate around 20 tiny tooth surfaces while a small person actively sabotages you.

Even the "smart" ones just added timers and songs. Like putting Bluetooth in a horse carriage.

Then I found something different. U shaped toothbrushes. The entire mouth, simultaneously. Thirty seconds total.

My first thought: This is either revolutionary or complete garbage.

I dove into the research. Most were garbage. Cheap silicone things from dropshippers. The reviews were savage, dentists calling them "expensive mouth toys."

But buried in a dental journal from Switzerland, I found something interesting.

The Swiss have been using U shaped ultrasonic cleaners in dental offices for years. Same concept as ultrasonic jewelry cleaners. High frequency vibrations create microscopic bubbles that implode and blast away debris.

The technology works. In professional settings. With $30,000 machines.

The consumer versions failed because they used weak motors and soft silicone to hit a $39 price point. Like putting a lawn mower engine in a Ferrari chassis.

But what if someone built one correctly? Real bristles. Actual ultrasonic frequency. Proper engineering.

I kept searching.

At 1:47 AM, I found it. A small company in Boston. Founded by an MIT engineer whose autistic son couldn't handle traditional brushing. They'd spent three years developing a U shaped brush that actually worked.

The specs were insane. 48,000 sonic vibrations per minute, the same frequency dental hygienists use. Nylon bristles at the precise angle for biofilm disruption. Medical grade construction.

It cost $189.

My Dyson hairdryer cost more.

I ordered two immediately.

They arrived three days later in packaging that looked like it contained medical equipment. Which, essentially, it did.

That night, I didn't tell the kids anything was different. Just handed them these sleek, U shaped devices.

"Put it in your mouth and press the button."

Parker looked suspicious. Olivia shrugged and complied.

Thirty seconds later, they were done.

Actually done. Not "pretend done" where I had to go back and fix their work. Not "mostly done except the back molars." Done done.

I made them do it again just to be sure. Another thirty seconds. The water they spit out was clean.

The next morning took 11 minutes. Total. Both kids dressed, teeth cleaned, hair brushed, downstairs for breakfast.

I'd reclaimed 7 minutes every morning, 4 minutes every night. That's 66 hours annually. Nearly three full days.

---

Notes on what these examples demonstrate: the extremely specific, checkable numbers throughout (Hopkins-style specificity); a single reveal mechanism (regulatory efficacy standards / ultrasonic frequency engineering) that reframes everything the reader already tried as structurally doomed; a hook built from a jarring, oddly specific claim; and a close that returns to the reader's own situation without ever issuing a command.
